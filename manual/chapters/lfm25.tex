\chapter{LFM2.5-24B-A2B}\label{ch:lfm25}

A decode token of LiquidAI's LFM2.5-24B-A2B reads 4.91~GB of its BF16 weights and 1.31~GB of our 4.0-bit EXL3 pack (\arith, from the headers), because only 4 of its 64 experts run in each of 38 layers. On one DGX Spark the engine decodes it at 145.8~tok/s from the 4.0 pack, 158.7~tok/s from the 3.0 pack and 44.7~tok/s in BF16 (\measured, 2026-10-14). It is the third model TandemLLM serves and the first that is not a Qwen. This chapter says what is different about it and what is measured; Chapter~\ref{ch:adding-model} describes the adapter path it came in through, and \code{docs/lfm2.5-24b-a2b.md} is the same material for the repository.

A short-convolution layer projects the normed input to three vectors B, C and x of 2,048, takes B times x, runs a depthwise convolution of 3 taps over that product along the sequence, multiplies by C and projects back. Its state is the last two products, so a sequence keeps 30 layers times 2 times 2,048 values. The router takes the sigmoid of each expert's logit, adds a per-expert bias to choose the 4, and renormalises the 4 chosen sigmoids. \code{engine/models/lfm2_moe/config.py} reads the configuration and stops on a field it does not know.

The tokenizer is LiquidAI's own (128,000 ids). The lookup corpus and the suffix store of the Qwen models hold other token ids, so the server refuses them by fingerprint and the LFM2.5 profiles keep a store of their own.

Every profile fits the Spark preset at the model's whole context: its KV is 2.5~GiB at 131,072 tokens. The quality numbers are exllamav3's \code{model_diff} against the BF16 checkpoint on 20 rows of 2,048 tokens of oasst1 chat; this engine's own comparison (\code{tools/lfm_quality.py}, 100 rows) agrees within 0.02. They are high next to exllamav3 conversions of other models at the same rates: the same recipe on the 8B sibling at 4.10 bits came within 0.01 of a reference conversion, so the 24B is harder at the same rate, for reasons not tested yet. The default is \code{lfm25-exl3-3.5}: the fastest profile in chat as it serves (the lookup on) whose chat top-1 agreement is at least 85~\% (86.7~\% at 143.7~tok/s; 3.0 is faster at 83.6~\%).

Below 4 bits a weight, exllamav3 1.6.0 gives some tensors half-integer rates (1.5, 2.5 or 3.5 bits; a tile is $16K + 8$ int16 wide). In such a tile an odd weight takes one bit more than an even one, so weight $i$'s 16-bit window ends at bit $(i+1)K_a + \lfloor (i+1)/2 \rfloor$ of the tile's ring, $K_a$ the whole part of the rate. The engine's kernels decode these tiles as exllamav3 does, in the decode step, a verify block and the prefill.

\section{One row path and exact rollback}

The decode step and every row of a verify block run the same kernels a row at a time (\code{engine/models/lfm2_moe/kernels.py}): the norms, the short convolution over the row's own window, q and k with their rotary, attention over the row's visible keys in fixed chunks of 512 keys, and the router. The EXL3 projections run on \code{tools/fn_kernels.dense} and the experts on its MoE bank kernels, which Flash-Next uses too; the BF16 projections run on a 16-row tile GEMM. So a row of a verify block has the bits of the decode step at that position. The load-time self-check proves it on the device: a recorded decode of 24 tokens verified as one block, every row bit for bit, before speculation turns on.

A verify block writes nothing into the convolution state. The commit takes the new state from the last two inputs along the accepted path, which are copies of the block's own rows, and moves the path's KV rows down. A rollback is exact by construction.

\section{Speed}

One request, greedy, the decode step from its CUDA graph runs at 188.2~tok/s on 2.5, 145.9 on 4.0 (191~GB/s of the bytes a token reads) and 44.7 in BF16 (220~GB/s), with dense splits measured at each pack's shapes (\code{ops/lfm25-dense-splits.json}; 4.0 ran at 136.1 without them). Launching the step's kernels one by one costs 5~\%. An 8,192-token prompt prefills at 7,360~tok/s on 4.0 (\measured).

A verify block costs about 3~ms a row more, because each row routes to its own experts and a block reads the union of them (Table~\ref{tab:lfm-stair}). A block pays only when it is accepted for about 1.4 tokens a row it adds.

The model has no draft layer, and Liquid will publish a drafter for it. Until then the server drafts with the lookup alone, through the shared tree drafter with no head (\code{NoHead}) and cut by StairCut with the staircase as its prior. With no head, each round's own lookup match decides through the cut's calibrated acceptance: a round with no match is the plain step and is priced as one, and the class-wide skip that a head needs is not consulted. On 4.0 with the store off, an edit ask ran at 291.1~tok/s (11.1 tokens a block) and a copy ask at 326.4~tok/s (13.5), against 141.3 and 141.9 plain, while the other three asks ran as plain, every text equal to the plain run's (\measured). Before the no-head pricing, the same asks in one server never drafted. Through the server on 4.0 the lookup also gave the same answer as plain decoding for all 20 requests of \code{tools/fn_server_check.py}, with a largest logprob difference of 0.0.

\section{Serving}

The chat template is the checkpoint's own and opens a reasoning block at the end of every prompt; a client that turns thinking off gets the block closed right after the generation prompt. Tool calls are a Python list between \code{<|tool_call_start|>} and \code{<|tool_call_end|>}; \code{server/toolcall.py} rewrites each block into the parser's JSON form as the text arrives, with values read as Python literals and never run, so a client gets OpenAI \code{tool_calls}. Liquid's recommended sampling is the default: temperature 0.1, \code{top_k} 50, repetition penalty 1.05. The Setup Guide turns the conductor off for this model: on four short chat asks its notes (1,241 to 2,714 hidden tokens) made the model think 856 to 1,437 tokens where it thought 99 to 902 without them, and with thinking off it reasoned in the answer instead of answering (\measured, 4.0).

The resident prefix and the state store keep the state through the adapter. A warm server's answers, with the prefix resumed at 4,096 and 12,288 tokens, equalled a cold server's with equal logprobs (\code{tools/fn_cache_check.py}, \measured). The profiles serve three slots over the one copy of the weights, and a slot's plain step joins the other slots' round as one row, from a graph when every slot takes one. On 4.0, 1, 2 and 3 requests at once decoded 139.9, 169.1 and 177.8~tok/s in total, every answer equal to its request alone (\measured). The session spill drains the resident conversation at a stop and reads it back at the next start (13,650 tokens in 309 and 163~ms), and the disk tier restores a prefix written after two cold prefills (12,288 tokens in 0.96 against 2.6~s cold), each with the cold server's answer (\measured); the profiles serve both.
